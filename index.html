A KID'S GUIDE TO THE HEART

Your heart is already your hardest worker.

It's been beating since before you were born — about 100,000 times a day — and it never takes a break. Let's find out how it does that, and what happens when a doctor hears something unusual.

Meet the muscle inside you

Your heart is a muscle about the size of your own fist, sitting just left of the middle of your chest. It's split into four rooms, called chambers — two on top, two on bottom. Blood flows through them in one direction only, pushed along by one-way doors called valves.

The top two rooms collect blood coming in. The bottom two rooms squeeze hard and pump it back out — one side sends blood to your lungs to pick up oxygen, the other side sends that fresh, oxygen-full blood everywhere else in your body.

What's a heart murmur?

A normal heartbeat sounds like "lub-DUB" through a stethoscope — that's the valves closing as blood moves through the chambers. A murmur is an extra sound in between, often described as a soft whooshing or swishing.

It usually just means blood is moving through the heart in a slightly different way than average — sometimes because a valve isn't quite fully formed, or a small opening is still closing up. Doctors find murmurs with a stethoscope, often at a routine checkup, even when a kid feels completely fine.

Most murmurs found in childhood are called "innocent" murmurs — the heart is working just fine, and the sound alone isn't something to be scared of. A doctor can tell the difference with a simple, painless test like an echocardiogram (an ultrasound of the heart).

My heart, my story

I was born with a heart murmur and a 2mm Ventricular Septal Defect, or VSD — a tiny hole between two chambers of the heart. It sounds scary, but it's actually one of the most common heart conditions there is. For a long time, though, nobody explained it to me in a way that made sense. I built Hearts for Hearts because I want kids today to get the heart education I never had.

Born with a 2mm VSD

Doctors found a heart murmur right away and diagnosed a small VSD — a tiny opening between the heart's chambers. It's extremely common, and most, like mine, close up on their own over time.

Checkups, slowly spacing out

At first I had checkups every 2 months. As the doctors watched things stay steady, that stretched to every 6 months, then once a year, then every 2 years — each step a quiet sign that things were heading in the right direction.

Starting to understand, around age 8

I only had a rough idea of what was actually going on with my heart. I knew I went to "heart checkups," but not really why, or what a VSD even was.

Cured at 12

By age 12, my VSD had closed and I was cured. Looking back, I wish someone had explained it to me in kid-friendly words the whole way through — instead of me just going along with checkups I didn't fully understand.

Why I built this

If you're a kid with a murmur, a VSD, or any heart condition your doctor mentioned, you deserve to actually understand what's happening in your own chest — not just show up to appointments and hope for the best.

Taking care of your heart

Move your body every day

Running, biking, swimming, dancing — anything that gets your heart beating faster for a while makes it stronger, just like exercising any other muscle.

Eat colorful food

Fruits, vegetables, and whole grains help keep the blood vessels around your heart clear and flexible.

Get enough sleep

Sleep is when your heart rate slows down and rests. Kids need about 9–12 hours a night, depending on age.

Ask questions at checkups

If a doctor mentions a sound, a rhythm, or anything about your heart, it's always okay to ask what it means in words that make sense to you.
